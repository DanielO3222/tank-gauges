Chapter 1

How to Use This Book & the First 72 Hours

Who this book is for

This is the survival handbook for our family in Wiggins, Colorado (Morgan County, eastern plains, about 4,500 feet). It is written for:

  • Daniel and his wife
  • The kids: a newborn (breastfeeding, born late September 2026), a 1-year-old (on formula), a 2-year-old (on regular milk), and a 6-year-old
  • Grandma and Grandpa, next door

We have chickens (about 6–12 eggs a day), many rabbits, a propane tank feeding a modern gas furnace and central AC, and an old wood stove in the basement that is not hooked up yet but we do have a chimney.

Our biggest local threats are blizzards and deep cold, long power outages, high wind, tornadoes and hail, grass fires, summer heat and drought, and flooding along the South Platte.

How to read it

  • Read it once, all the way through, now while times are normal. Most of what keeps a family alive happens before an emergency.
  • Each chapter ends with a "Do it now" checklist. Check things off in pencil.
  • The Home Worksheet (the Appendix at the end of this book) is where we write down our shutoffs, propane, well, chimney, medicines, and contacts. Fill it in and keep it updated.
  • Keep one printed copy in the kitchen and one at Grandma and Grandpa's. Put it in a gallon zip bag so it survives water.
  • Phones die. Paper doesn't. If the power is out, use the paper copy.

Rule of thumb: This book is a guide, not a doctor, electrician, or chimney sweep. Use professionals now, while they are available. If services are running and you're worried about a baby, call the doctor or 911. If they aren't, Chapters 8 and 9 are written for that.

The rule of threes (what kills first)

People usually die in this order when things go bad:

  1. Air / carbon monoxide — minutes. (Generators, grills, or heaters used indoors.)
  2. Cold (exposure) — hours in a Colorado blizzard. Babies and older adults lose heat fastest.
  3. Water — about 3 days. Babies dehydrate much faster.
  4. Food — weeks.

So the priority list is always: safe air → warmth → water → food → everything else.

Survival mindset (from the U.S. Army Survival Manual)

The Army's survival manual (FM 21-76, now ATP 3-50.21) builds everything on the word SURVIVAL:

  • Size up the situation, yourself, and your gear.
  • Use all your senses — and Undue haste makes waste. Don't rush into mistakes.
  • Remember where you are (and where help, water, and fuel are).
  • Vanquish fear and panic.
  • Improvise.
  • Value living — stubbornness to get the family through matters.
  • Act like the natives — learn how locals (old ranchers, Grandma and Grandpa) handled it.
  • Live by your wits, but for now, learn basic skills.

When something goes wrong, use STOP: Stop, Think, Observe, Plan — before acting.

Lessons from people who lived it

  • Texas, February 2021 (Winter Storm Uri): millions lost power for days in deep cold. Families learned that no power means no heat and often no water, pipes burst, and diesel fuel can gel in very cold weather (keep anti-gel additive in diesel trucks and generators). Many died of hypothermia and carbon monoxide from improvised heating. (The Prepared; CDC)
  • The 1888 "Schoolhouse Blizzard" on the Great Plains hit on a mild day with almost no warning; many people, including children walking home from school, died caught outside. Lesson: when a plains blizzard hits, stay put in shelter.
  • Eastern Colorado, December 2006: two blizzards struck about a week apart. See Chapter 12.

The first hour

  1. Get everyone together (both houses). Count heads: 8 people.
  2. Check for danger: gas smell, fire, injuries, downed power lines. If you smell gas: get out, don't flip switches, call 911 / the propane company from outside.
  3. Turn on the battery NOAA Weather Radio (Chapter 10) to learn what is happening.
  4. Fill water containers and bathtubs now while water pressure still works (Chapter 2).
  5. Decide: stay or go? Most of the time we shelter in place. Leave if: fire is approaching, floodwater is rising, the house is unsafe, or officials order evacuation.

The first 24 hours

  • Pick one warm room if heat is out (Chapter 3). Close off the rest of the house.
  • Keep the fridge and freezer shut. A full freezer holds food safely about 48 hours, a half-full one about 24 hours, if the door stays closed. A fridge holds about 4 hours. (USDA)
  • Check on babies every hour: warm? feeding? wet diapers?
  • Ration phone battery. Text instead of calling. Turn on low-power mode.
  • Check animals: water for chickens and rabbits. In winter, it freezes fast.
  • Write down what happened and when on the log page at the back.

The first 72 hours

  • Make a supply count (Chapter 13 inventory sheets): water, formula, diapers, food, propane %, firewood, medicines.
  • Set a routine: meals, baby feeds, chores, sleep shifts, someone on watch for fire/CO.
  • Combine households if heat or water is short. Two houses heated is twice the fuel. Usually it is better for everyone to live in one house.
  • Start conserving immediately. It is easier to ease up later than to run out.

If we must leave (evacuation)

Keep a go-bag for each person, ready by the door (see Chapter 13). Pick meeting places:

  • Spot 1: End of our driveway (fire in the house).
  • Spot 2: A place in town (fill in on the worksheet).
  • Spot 3: Out of the area — a relative or friend (fill in).

Leave a note on the door saying where we went and when.

Do it now

  • Read this whole book once.
  • Fill in the Home Worksheet.
  • Print two copies; one at each house.
  • Buy a battery/hand-crank NOAA Weather Radio.
  • Put battery CO alarms on every level of both houses.
  • Sign up for Morgan County emergency alerts (ask the County Sheriff's Office / Office of Emergency Management) and turn on Wireless Emergency Alerts on all phones.

Water

You can live weeks without food but only about 3 days without water — and babies, nursing moms, and older adults much less. Water is the first thing to secure.

How much water we need

The standard rule (FEMA / Ready.gov): at least 1 gallon per person per day for drinking and basic hygiene. Store at least a 2-week supply if you can.

Nursing mothers, sick people, and anyone in hot weather need more. Plan extra.

Who Gallons/day (minimum) 2 weeks
7 people eating/drinking (all but newborn) 7 98
Extra for nursing mom, formula, cooking, heat +2 +28
Family total ~9 ~126 gallons
Chickens & rabbits see Chapters 5–6

Our goal: about 125+ gallons stored for people, plus water for animals.

Storing water

  • Use food-grade containers: store-bought bottled water, 5–7 gallon water jugs, or 55-gallon food-grade barrels (blue).
  • Do not use milk jugs (they break down and leak) or containers that held chemicals.
  • Store in a cool, dark place, off concrete (put on a pallet or wood).
  • Replace stored tap water every 6 months. Unopened commercial bottled water: follow the date. (Ready.gov)
  • Don't let it freeze and split — keep inside the heated part of the house in winter, or leave room for expansion.
  • Hidden water in the house: the water heater tank (often 40–50 gallons), pipes, and toilet tanks (not bowls; and not if you use tank cleaners).

Draining the water heater in an emergency

  1. Turn off the gas/power to the water heater first.
  2. Close the cold-water inlet valve on top.
  3. Open a hot faucet upstairs to let air in.
  4. Drain from the valve at the bottom into a clean container. The first water may have sediment — let it settle.

Making water safe

If the water might be contaminated (well flooded, town "boil water" notice, river or stock tank water):

1. Boil (best)

  • Let cloudy water settle, then filter it through a clean cloth or coffee filter.
  • Bring to a rolling boil for 1 minute. (Above 6,500 ft boil 3 minutes; Wiggins is below that.) (CDC)
  • Let cool. Pour between clean containers to improve taste.

2. Bleach (when you can't boil)

Use plain, unscented household bleach (no "splash-less" or scented). Check the label for the percent.

Bleach strength Per 1 gallon of clear water Per 5 gallons
6% 8 drops (about 1/8 tsp) 40 drops (½ tsp)
8.25% 6 drops 30 drops (about ⅓ tsp)
  • Double the dose if the water is cloudy or very cold.
  • Stir and wait 30 minutes. It should smell slightly of chlorine. If not, repeat and wait another 15 minutes. (EPA)
  • Bleach loses strength over time — replace yearly.

3. Filters

A good gravity or squeeze filter (such as those rated to remove bacteria and protozoa) is very useful, but most filters do NOT remove viruses or chemicals. For the safest water: filter, then boil or bleach.

What none of these fix

Chemicals, fuel, fertilizer, or pesticides (common after floods in farm country) are not removed by boiling or bleach. If water smells of fuel or chemicals, don't use it.

Water for the babies

  • The newborn needs no water — only breast milk. Never give a young baby plain water.
  • Formula water: use bottled water or water that has been boiled. If unsure of water safety, use ready-to-feed formula (needs no water at all) — this is the best emergency choice. See Chapter 4.
  • Wash bottles in safe water; boil bottles and nipples if you can't wash properly.

If we have a private well

Most wells use an electric submersible pump. No power = no water. Plan one of these now:

  1. Generator + transfer switch or interlock installed by a licensed electrician so the well pump can run. Well pumps often need a large generator — check the pump's label (volts/horsepower) and ask the electrician.
  2. Manual well hand pump installed alongside the electric pump. Several brands make pumps that share the well casing. Whether this works depends on our static water level — deep water levels need special (and more expensive) pumps. Get our well log (worksheet) and ask a licensed well contractor.
  3. Solar well pump (costly, but works for years).
  4. Stored water (above) to bridge the gap.

After a flood: a flooded well may be contaminated. Don't drink it until it is disinfected (shock chlorinated) and tested. Colorado labs and CSU Extension give guidance; the county health department can help.

Freezing: keep the well house/pressure tank area above freezing — insulate, use a heat lamp only if fire-safe, and keep the pump area dry.

If we have town water

  • Town water usually keeps flowing for a while in outages, but pressure may drop and boil-water notices may come. Listen to the radio.
  • Fill everything when trouble starts.
  • Know where the main shutoff is — if a pipe breaks, shut it off fast.

Winter: keeping pipes from freezing

  • Keep the house above freezing. Open cabinet doors under sinks on outside walls.
  • Let a faucet drip on very cold nights (if water supply allows).
  • If we lose heat for days: shut off the main water, open all faucets (top floor first, then lowest), and flush toilets to drain lines. Put a little RV antifreeze in drains and toilet bowls if you have it. (Do not drink RV antifreeze.)
  • Snow is water: melt it — but treat/boil it like any untreated water, and it takes a lot of snow (and fuel) to make a little water. Never eat snow to stay hydrated — it chills the body.

Sanitation (just as important as water)

Dirty hands and human waste spread diarrhea, which can kill babies quickly through dehydration.

  • Wash hands with soap and water after the toilet, diapers, animals, and before food. Hand sanitizer (60%+ alcohol) if water is short — but it doesn't work well on dirty hands.
  • If toilets can't flush: use a bucket toilet: a 5-gallon bucket lined with two heavy trash bags. Add kitty litter, sawdust, or wood ash after each use. Seal and store bags outside away from water and animals. Keep a lid on.
  • Don't put human waste or used diapers in the chicken or garden compost.
  • Keep the bucket toilet away from the kitchen and water storage.
  • Dish water: wash dishes in hot soapy water, rinse in water with a little bleach (about 1 tablespoon bleach per gallon of water), air-dry.

River and stock water

The South Platte and ditches near farm fields can carry farm runoff and animal waste. Only use them if you must — settle, filter, then boil. Never let kids play in floodwater.

Do it now

  • Store ~125 gallons of drinking water (start with cases of bottled water and a few 5–7 gal jugs).
  • Buy unscented bleach and an eyedropper; tape the dose chart to the jug.
  • Buy a water filter (bacteria + protozoa) and a large stockpot for boiling.
  • Learn how to drain our water heater.
  • Well: get the well log; talk to an electrician (generator inlet) and a well contractor (hand pump).
  • Bucket toilet kit: 2 buckets with lids, heavy bags, kitty litter, TP, gloves.
  • Mark the main water shutoff with a tag.

Chapter 3

Heat & Shelter in Winter

On the eastern plains, a winter storm with wind can drop wind chills far below zero. Without heat, a house can become dangerously cold within a day or two. Babies and Grandma and Grandpa are at highest risk. This chapter covers keeping warm with propane, with the wood stove, and with neither.

⚠ Carbon monoxide (CO) is one of the biggest killers in winter power outages. It has no smell. Every year families die from generators, grills, and heaters used indoors. Put battery-powered CO alarms on every level, and near every sleeping area, in both houses — now.

Our heat sources, in order

  1. Propane furnace (normal) — needs electricity to run.
  2. Wood stove (basement) — reconnected to our existing chimney, after professional inspection.
  3. Indoor-rated propane heater (backup, used exactly per the label).
  4. Body heat, layers, and one insulated room (always works).

1. The propane furnace

No power = no furnace

A modern gas furnace uses electricity for the igniter, controls, and blower fan. When the power is out, it will not run even with a full propane tank.

Fix (do this now, if we choose a generator): have a licensed electrician install a generator inlet with a transfer switch or interlock, so a generator outside can power the furnace (and well pump, fridge, lights). Never "backfeed" a generator through a dryer plug — it can kill line workers and burn the house.

Making propane last

  • One gallon of propane holds about 91,500 BTU of heat.
  • Read the tank gauge (percent full). Gallons left ≈ tank size × gauge % ÷ 100. Example: 500-gal tank at 60% ≈ 300 gallons.
  • Write the reading daily in the log (Chapter 13). After 3 days we'll know our burn rate: gallons used per day → days left.
  • Conserve:
  • Set the thermostat lower (around 60–62°F) in the house, and keep the babies' room warmer.
  • Close vents and doors to unused rooms.
  • Cover windows at night (blankets, bubble wrap, foil-faced foam board); open south windows on sunny days.
  • Roll towels against door gaps.
  • Shut off the propane water heater if not needed (heat water on the stove for washing instead).
  • Keep the tank lid, regulator, and path to the tank clear of snow so it can be refilled and the regulator vent doesn't block.
  • Order propane early in the fall — in a crisis, delivery may stop. Don't let it go below about 30% going into winter storms.

2. Reconnecting the wood stove to our chimney

We have a chimney and an old stove in the basement. This is a great backup — but only if it is done right. A bad connection can cause a chimney fire, house fire, or CO poisoning. Do it now, while professionals are available, so it's ready when we need it.

Step 1 — Inspect the stove

  • Look for a metal label on the back (UL or other listing, brand, model, required clearances). If it has a label, the label and owner's manual rule.
  • Check for cracks in the firebox (cast iron or steel), warped plates, missing firebricks, broken door glass, worn door gaskets (should seal tight — test by closing the door on a dollar bill; it should be hard to pull out).
  • If cracked or badly worn: replace it. A new EPA-certified stove burns cleaner, uses less wood, and makes less creosote. (EPA Burn Wise)

Step 2 — Get the chimney inspected and swept

Hire a CSIA-certified chimney sweep (Chimney Safety Institute of America) for a Level 2 inspection (they often use a camera). They will check:

  • Cracks, missing mortar, or blockages (bird nests are common).
  • Creosote buildup — the black, tarry fuel that causes chimney fires.
  • The flue liner. Many older masonry chimneys have cracked clay tiles or no liner at all. Stoves often need a stainless-steel liner sized to the stove's flue outlet (for example a 6-inch stove → a 6-inch liner). An oversized flue drafts poorly and builds creosote fast.
  • Is this flue shared with the furnace or water heater? A wood stove must have its own flue unless the manufacturer and code specifically allow otherwise. Many propane appliances vent through the chimney — the sweep must check this.
  • Chimney height: the top should be at least 3 ft above where it exits the roof and 2 ft higher than anything within 10 ft (the "3-2-10 rule"). Too short = poor draft and smoke.
  • Cap and spark arrestor — important on the windy, dry plains.

Step 3 — The connection (stovepipe/connector)

  • The pipe from the stove to the chimney is called the connector. It enters the chimney through a thimble (a clay or metal sleeve set in the masonry).
  • Single-wall stovepipe needs 18 inches clearance from combustibles (wood, drywall, joists) unless the stove or pipe label says otherwise. Double-wall (close-clearance) pipe allows less — follow its label. (NFPA 211)
  • Keep the connector short, with few elbows. Horizontal runs should slope up toward the chimney (commonly about ¼ inch per foot).
  • Crimped (male) ends point down toward the stove so creosote drips back inside.
  • Fasten each joint with 3 sheet-metal screws.
  • Never run stovepipe through a wall, floor, or ceiling by itself. It must go through a listed thimble or wall pass-through.
  • Never connect into a fireplace opening without a proper liner and connection — the old fireplace flue is usually far too big and unsafe.

Step 4 — Clearances and floor

  • Labeled stove: use the clearances on the label/manual.
  • Unlabeled (unlisted) old stove: NFPA 211 requires large clearances — 36 inches from the stove to combustible walls/materials unless protected with approved shielding. Get the sweep or installer to confirm.
  • Floor: a noncombustible hearth pad (stone, tile on cement board, or a listed pad) sized per the manual. A concrete basement floor usually works — but check that nothing combustible is under or near it.
  • Nothing stored within 3 ft: no firewood stacks, laundry, kids' toys, or propane bottles.

Step 5 — Permits, insurance, test fire

  • Ask Morgan County building department if a permit/inspection is required.
  • Tell our home insurance — some require a professional install.
  • Draft test: with a window cracked, hold a lit match or incense near the open stove door — smoke should be pulled in. If it's not, warm the flue first by burning a twist of newspaper. If it still smokes into the room, stop and call the sweep.
  • Do a few small test fires with CO alarms running and someone watching.

Using the stove safely

  • Burn only dry, seasoned firewood (split and dried at least 6 months, under 20% moisture — a cheap moisture meter checks this). (EPA Burn Wise) Wet wood = smoke, little heat, lots of creosote.
  • Never burn: trash, plastics, treated or painted wood, pallets with "MB" stamp, cardboard boxes in bulk, or gasoline/charcoal lighter fluid to start it.
  • Keep a fireplace screen/safety gate around the stove — toddlers and the 6-year-old must never be near it. A stove can cause severe burns in a second.
  • Ashes: put in a metal can with a lid, set outside on dirt/concrete, away from the house. Ashes stay hot for days.
  • Sweep the chimney at least once a year; check monthly when burning heavily.
  • Chimney fire signs: loud roaring, popping, dense smoke, hot chimney. Close the stove air controls, get everyone out, call 911.
  • Keep an ABC fire extinguisher near the stove and in the kitchen.

Firewood on the plains

Trees are scarce around Wiggins, so stock up now. Firewood is sold by the cord (a stack 4 ft × 4 ft × 8 ft = 128 cubic feet). How much we need depends on the house, stove, and winter — heating a whole house all winter can take several cords. Heating one room as an emergency backup takes much less. Track it: note how much one cold week uses, then multiply.

  • Store wood off the ground, covered on top, open on the sides, at least 30 ft from the house when possible (fire safety), with a few days' supply closer.
  • Scrap lumber (untreated), downed cottonwood along the river (with landowner permission), and tree trimmings can supplement.

If there were no chimney

(Not our case — we have one.) Never improvise a vent out a window or wall. A stove with no chimney needs a professionally installed Class A insulated chimney pipe.

3. Backup propane heaters

  • Only use heaters labeled for indoor use, with an oxygen depletion sensor (ODS) and tip-over shutoff. Follow the manual about ventilation (most require a cracked window).
  • Use with a CO alarm, never while sleeping, never near curtains or kids.
  • Never use for heat indoors: gas oven or stovetop, charcoal or propane grills, camp stoves, patio heaters, kerosene "torpedo" heaters, or a generator. (CDC)
  • Store 1-lb and 20-lb propane cylinders outside, never in the house or basement.

4. No heat at all — the "one warm room" plan

  1. Choose one room — ideally an interior room with few windows, near the wood stove if it is running (the basement can work well: the earth around it holds steady temperature, and it's also our tornado shelter).
  2. Move everyone in, including Grandma and Grandpa. Shared body heat matters.
  3. Insulate it: hang blankets over doors and windows, put rugs/cardboard on the floor, tape plastic sheeting over windows.
  4. Make a "tent" inside the room: a table covered with blankets, or a small camping tent, holds heat around sleepers.
  5. Layers: wool or synthetic, not cotton. Hats, dry socks, mittens. Change damp clothes right away.
  6. Eat and drink: the body makes heat from food. Warm drinks help (no alcohol — it makes hypothermia worse).
  7. Babies: see Chapter 4 for safe warmth. Never put a baby to sleep with loose blankets, pillows, or in an adult bed.
  8. A tent inside the room (The Prepared, after the 2021 Texas blackout): set up a camping tent with its rainfly on, pile blankets over it, and sleep inside. A person gives off roughly 330 BTU of heat per hour — a small enclosed space traps it.
  9. Shovel paths often during a storm (to the propane tank, coop, hutches, woodpile) rather than all at once at the end.
  10. Hot water bottles (warm, not hot — wrap in a towel; never against a baby's skin).

Summer heat

Summers here can be very hot and dry. Babies and older adults overheat fast.

  • Keep the house closed and shaded in the day, open it at night.
  • Use the basement as the cool room.
  • Wet cloths, cool (not cold) baths for kids, plenty of fluids.
  • Never leave a child or pet in a car.
  • Heat stroke (hot, confused, not sweating) = 911.

Do it now

  • Battery CO alarms on every level and sleeping area, both houses. Smoke alarms tested.
  • CSIA-certified sweep: Level 2 chimney inspection + liner recommendation.
  • Inspect/repair or replace the stove; professional connection; ask county about permit; tell insurance.
  • Hearth pad, stove gate, metal ash can, ABC extinguisher, moisture meter.
  • Buy firewood (dry) and store it right.
  • Electrician: generator inlet + transfer switch/interlock (furnace, well, fridge).
  • Fill propane in early fall; record gauge.
  • Wool blankets, sleeping bags rated for cold, plastic sheeting, duct tape.

Chapter 4

Babies & Kids

Our kids are the most vulnerable people in the family. Babies get cold, dehydrated, and sick faster than adults, and they can't tell us. This chapter is deliberately careful. If services are running, call the pediatrician or 911 when in doubt. If they aren't, Chapter 9 covers caring for a sick baby at home.

Our kids at a glance

Kid Feeds on Biggest risks in an emergency
Newborn (born late Sep 2026) Breast milk only Cold, dehydration, fever/infection, unsafe sleep
1-year-old Formula → moving to whole milk Running out of formula/milk, choking, dehydration
2-year-old Regular milk + table food Dehydration from diarrhea, choking, burns (stove)
6-year-old Regular food Fear/stress, wandering, burns, cold exposure outdoors

The newborn — breastfeeding

Breastfeeding is the safest emergency food in the world: always the right temperature, no water needed, no bottles to clean, and it protects against infection. Protect Mom's ability to nurse.

  • Newborns typically nurse 8–12 times per 24 hours. (AAP) Keep nursing on demand, even during stress — the more baby nurses, the more milk is made.
  • Stress does not usually dry up milk, but it can slow let-down. Skin-to-skin, a warm quiet spot, and a calm helper help.
  • The newborn needs NO water, juice, tea, or food — only breast milk. Water can be dangerous for young babies.
  • Is baby getting enough? After the first week: about 6 or more wet diapers a day, regular stools, swallowing sounds while feeding, steady weight gain, alert when awake.
  • Get a manual breast pump (no power needed) and learn hand expression — in case Mom and baby are briefly apart, or Mom is engorged.
  • Pumped milk storage (CDC): room temperature up to 4 hours; refrigerator up to 4 days; freezer about 6 months best (up to 12).

Feeding Mom (very important)

  • A breastfeeding mother needs about 450–500 extra calories a day. (CDC) She eats first when food is short — her milk feeds the baby.
  • Drink to thirst — a simple habit is a full glass of water every time she nurses. Dark urine = drink more.
  • Keep taking a prenatal vitamin; stockpile several months.
  • Eggs and rabbit are great protein for her — add fat and carbs (see Chapter 6 on "rabbit starvation").
  • If Mom gets sick (cold, flu, stomach bug), she can usually keep breastfeeding — her antibodies pass to the baby. Check any medicine with the doctor/pharmacist or LactMed (a National Library of Medicine database).

Backup if Mom can't nurse

If Mom were injured, very ill, or separated, the newborn would need infant formula. Keep a small backup supply of ready-to-feed infant formula (no water needed) and disposable nipples — but don't use it routinely, because skipping feeds lowers Mom's milk supply. Ask the pediatrician which formula.

The 1-year-old — formula and moving to milk

  • The American Academy of Pediatrics says most children can switch from formula to whole cow's milk at about 12 months, about 16–24 oz (2–3 cups) a day, plus solid foods. Toddler/"transition" formulas are not needed. (AAP / HealthyChildren.org) Talk to the pediatrician about switching now, before an emergency — it makes us less dependent on formula.
  • Too much milk (over about 24 oz a day) can fill them up and lower iron. Offer iron-rich foods: eggs, meat (including rabbit), beans, iron-fortified cereal.

If still on formula — stockpile math

  1. Check the can: it says "makes about ___ fl oz."
  2. Daily ounces × days ÷ ounces per can = cans needed. Example: 24 oz/day × 30 days = 720 oz. If a can makes 90 oz → 8 cans for a month.
  3. Rotate: use the oldest first, watch expiration dates.

Formula safety — never break these rules

  • NEVER make homemade formula. It can cause serious illness and death. (FDA, AAP)
  • NEVER water down formula to stretch it. It can cause dangerous low sodium (seizures) and malnutrition. (AAP)
  • NEVER give an infant under 12 months cow's milk, goat milk, or plant milk as a formula substitute.
  • Mix exactly as the label says.
  • Use safe water (bottled, or boiled then cooled). Ready-to-feed is best when water is uncertain.
  • Throw out prepared formula after the time on the label (often 1 hour once baby starts drinking).

The 2-year-old — milk and food

  • Keeps drinking milk + eating family food. Ask the doctor whether to stay on whole milk or switch to lower-fat after age 2.
  • Shelf-stable milk: stock UHT (boxed) milk and powdered whole milk for when fresh milk runs out. Once opened, refrigerate/use quickly.

Safe foods from our farm for the little ones

  • Eggs: great food for all the kids except the newborn. Cook fully — firm yolks and whites — for young children. (Eggs can be introduced to babies around 6 months; the newborn is far from that.)
  • Rabbit meat: cook thoroughly; shred or mince finely; remove every small bone — rabbit bones are thin and sharp.
  • Choking hazards for under 4: whole grapes, nuts, popcorn, hot dog rounds, hard raw vegetables, chunks of meat, spoonfuls of peanut butter. Cut food into small, soft pieces.
  • No honey for babies under 1 year (botulism).
  • Wash hands after handling chickens or eggs — chickens commonly carry Salmonella. Kids under 5 should not kiss or snuggle chickens. (CDC)

Keeping babies warm (and safe)

  • Dress the baby in about one more layer than an adult would wear to be comfortable. (AAP)
  • Feel the chest or back of the neck — warm and dry is right. Cold hands/feet alone are normal; sweaty or flushed = too hot.
  • Skin-to-skin with an awake adult, covered by a blanket, is an excellent way to warm a baby.
  • Safe sleep, every sleep (AAP): on the back, on a firm, flat surface (crib, bassinet, or play yard) in the same room as parents, with no loose blankets, pillows, or bumpers. Use a wearable blanket/sleep sack and footed pajamas for warmth instead of loose blankets. Don't let baby sleep in a car seat outside the car, a swing, or an adult bed or couch.
  • Keep babies away from the wood stove and space heaters — and keep the sleep space away from them too.
  • A baby who is cold to the touch, unusually quiet, floppy, or not feeding = emergency.

Diapers and hygiene

  • Rough daily use: newborns about 8–12, older babies/toddlers fewer. Stock at least a month of each size the kids will be in (sizes change fast!).
  • Backup: cloth diapers (prefolds + covers) and a bucket with lid. Washing needs water and heat — plan for it.
  • Wipes, diaper cream, garbage bags, and hand sanitizer.
  • Bury or bag soiled diapers away from water and gardens; never in chicken compost.

Dehydration — know the signs

Kids can get dangerously dry from diarrhea, vomiting, fever, or heat.

Warning signs:

  • Fewer wet diapers (or none in 6+ hours for a baby), dark urine
  • Dry mouth, no tears when crying
  • Sunken soft spot on top of a baby's head, sunken eyes
  • Very sleepy, floppy, or very fussy

What to do: keep breastfeeding/feeding; give oral rehydration solution (store-bought such as Pedialyte) in small sips for kids over infancy as directed on the package and by the doctor. A newborn with any of these signs needs a doctor right away if one can be reached; if not, see Chapter 9 — feed often, give fluids by spoon, and track diapers.

Fever

  • Any baby under 3 months with a temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher is an emergency — if services are running, go to the ER immediately. (AAP) That is our newborn right now. With no help coming, follow "A fever in our newborn" in Chapter 9.
  • Get a digital thermometer (rectal is most accurate for babies).
  • Ask the pediatrician now for the correct dose of acetaminophen (and ibuprofen for 6+ months) by weight, and write it in the Home Worksheet. Never give aspirin to children.

The 6-year-old (and keeping kids steady)

  • Kids handle hard times better with routine, honest simple explanations, and jobs. Give the 6-year-old real jobs: gathering eggs, feeding rabbits, carrying kindling (supervised), being "radio helper."
  • Teach: our address, parents' names and phone numbers, the meeting places, "stay away from the stove and the road," and to never go outside in a blizzard alone.
  • Keep books, crayons, cards, and favorite toys in the emergency kit.
  • Watch for stress: bedwetting, nightmares, clinginess. It's normal — extra hugs and routine help.

Do it now

  • Ask the pediatrician: switching the 1-year-old to whole milk; medicine doses by weight; what to keep in a baby kit.
  • Manual breast pump + bottles + milk storage bags.
  • Small backup of ready-to-feed infant formula (newborn) + 1 month of the 1-year-old's formula/milk plan.
  • UHT boxed milk and powdered whole milk.
  • 1 month of diapers in the next sizes + cloth backup, wipes, cream.
  • Prenatal vitamins (3+ months) for Mom.
  • Digital thermometer, infant/children's acetaminophen, oral rehydration solution.
  • Wearable blankets/sleep sacks, footed pajamas, a portable play yard for the warm room.

Food: Eggs & Chickens

Our hens give 6–12 eggs a day — that's real food: each large egg has about 70 calories and 6 grams of high-quality protein (USDA). Nine eggs a day ≈ 630 calories and 55 g protein for the family. The challenge is keeping the hens fed and laying when we can't buy feed, especially in winter.

How much hens eat and drink

  • A laying hen eats roughly ¼ lb of feed a day (more in cold weather). (University Extension, typical figure)
  • 15 hens ≈ 3¾ lb/day ≈ 110 lb per month.
  • A hen drinks roughly a pint of water a day, more in heat. No water = no eggs within a day or two.
  • Laying hens need feed of about 16% protein plus calcium for strong shells. (Extension)

Stockpile now: several months of layer feed in metal cans with lids (mice!), plus oyster shell. Feed goes stale — rotate, use oldest first, keep dry. Never feed moldy feed.

Why hens stop laying in winter

  1. Short days. Hens lay best with about 14–16 hours of light. In December, Wiggins gets only about 9½ hours of daylight. (Extension)
  2. Molting in fall — they stop to regrow feathers (needs extra protein).
  3. Cold stress, not enough feed or water.
  4. Age — production drops after the second year.

What helps:

  • Add light to make about 14 hours total: a low-watt LED on a timer, turned on early morning (so they go to roost at natural dusk). If power is out, a small solar light kit can do this.
  • Raise protein during molt (insects, cooked egg, meat scraps — see below).
  • Keep water unfrozen. Heated waterer base if power; otherwise swap waterers 2–3 times a day with warm water.
  • Plan to replace hens: hatch or raise new pullets each spring so we always have young layers. A rooster lets us hatch our own (a broody hen or incubator).

Winter coop care on the plains

  • Ventilation without drafts: vents high up, no wind blowing on roosting birds. Damp air causes frostbite on combs more than cold does.
  • Deep litter: keep adding dry straw/pine shavings; it composts slowly and adds a little warmth.
  • Avoid heat lamps — they are a leading cause of coop and barn fires. Chickens handle cold well if dry and out of the wind.
  • Wind block: straw bales, tarps, or plywood on the north and west sides.
  • Close them in securely at night: coyotes, foxes, raccoons, skunks, and owls are all around Wiggins. Hardware cloth, not chicken wire.

The "Raken" house — rabbits over chickens (Joel Salatin)

At Polyface Farm, Joel Salatin's family hangs rabbit cages above a floor where hens run — the "Raken" (rabbit + chicken) house. Rabbit droppings and urine fall into deep carbon bedding (wood shavings, sawdust, leaves, chopped straw). The hens scratch it constantly, which keeps it from smelling, turns it into compost, and breeds worms and bugs that the hens eat — free protein all winter.

Salatin's deep-bedding rules:

  • Start with at least 8 inches, preferably 12+, of carbon bedding.
  • Add more carbon whenever it smells. Long hay/straw mats — chop it or add a little at a time.
  • A dirt floor drains best. Good ventilation, especially in warm weather.
  • Toss scratch grain on the bedding to get the hens working it.
  • The finished compost is excellent garden fertilizer in spring.

This fits our place perfectly: it puts Daniel's two animals together so the rabbits help feed the chickens without killing a single rabbit, and it keeps both in one sheltered, easier-to-water building in winter. (Keep rabbit cages high and solid enough that hens can't roost on them and soil the rabbits.)

Feeding chickens when feed runs out

Chickens are omnivores — they eat grains, greens, seeds, bugs, and meat. Mix several of these:

Grains (energy)

  • Whole or cracked wheat, corn, milo (sorghum), barley, oats. We live in grain country — buy or trade directly from local farmers and elevators now, by the bag or ton. Grain stores for years if dry and sealed.
  • Corn alone is low in protein — hens will lay poorly on corn only. Add protein.

Protein (eggs need protein)

  • Insects (see the bug farms below).
  • Cooked eggs (scrambled, with shell crushed in) — especially cracked or extra eggs.
  • Cooked rabbit scraps and organs (see below).
  • Fish scraps, cooked; milk/whey or cottage cheese in small amounts.
  • Dried peas or other legumes — cooked. Raw dry beans are toxic.

Calcium (shells)

  • Oyster shell free-choice; or our own eggshells — rinse, bake dry, crush fine, offer in a separate dish.

Greens (vitamins, especially in winter)

  • Stored pumpkins, squash, cabbage, beets, carrots (grow extras in summer for the chickens).
  • Alfalfa hay leaves (shake the leafy bits out of a bale).
  • Sprouted grain "fodder": soak wheat or barley overnight, spread in trays with drain holes, rinse twice a day, feed in about 6–8 days when it's a green mat. Honest note: sprouting does not add calories (it uses some up), but it gives fresh greens and vitamins in winter and hens love it. Needs room temperature and water.

Kitchen scraps

  • OK: vegetable and fruit scraps, cooked rice/pasta/oats, bread (small amounts), cooked meat scraps, garden waste.
  • Not OK: moldy or rotten food, avocado (pits/skins), raw or dried beans, chocolate, very salty food, green potato peels, onions in large amounts, coffee grounds.

Bug farms — protein we can raise indoors in winter

Mealworms (easiest for winter, indoors)

  • Plastic bins (with ventilation holes) of wheat bran or rolled oats a few inches deep.
  • Add slices of carrot or potato for moisture (replace before mold).
  • Keep at room temperature — the life cycle (beetle → egg → worm → pupa → beetle) takes a few months, slower when cool.
  • Start in late summer so the colony is big by winter. Harvest larger worms; leave enough to breed.
  • Start with a few thousand bought live mealworms.

Black soldier fly larvae (BSFL) — best in summer

  • Larvae eat almost any food waste and manure and are high in protein and fat. The mature larvae crawl out on their own into a bucket ("self-harvesting" bins).
  • They need warmth (they slow down a lot in cool weather) — great from late spring to early fall outdoors. Over winter, they're hard to keep without a heated space. Plan for summer production and dry the extra larvae (in an oven or dehydrator) to store for winter.

Earthworms (red wigglers)

  • A worm bin with shredded cardboard, kitchen scraps, and rabbit manure (rabbit manure is great for worms). Keep it above freezing — basement works.

Daniel's idea: rabbits as chicken food

Yes — this works, with the right approach. Chickens love meat and it's real protein, especially for winter laying and molting.

Do it this way:

  • We eat the rabbit meat. Feed the chickens the parts we don't eat: organs we're not using, heads, scraps, fat trimmings, and bones (cooked, then cracked).
  • Cook it first. Boil scraps and organs until fully cooked. Cooking kills parasites and germs (including tularemia, which rabbits can carry) and keeps the hens from developing a taste for raw meat that can lead to egg- or flock-pecking.
  • Never feed spoiled or sick-animal meat. Bury or burn any rabbit that died of illness.
  • Feed scraps the same day, a modest amount, alongside grain — not as the only food.

The energy trade-off (important): every time food passes through an animal, most of its energy is lost. A rabbit turns hay into meat inefficiently; a hen turns rabbit meat into eggs inefficiently. Feeding whole rabbits to chickens to get eggs yields far fewer calories than eating the rabbit ourselves. So the best plan is: people eat the meat; chickens get the leftovers. That's a win twice.

About the reverse: rabbits are strict plant-eaters — never feed chicken or any meat to rabbits.

Keeping eggs

  • Unwashed eggs straight from clean nests keep their natural coating. The USDA recommends refrigeration for safety; in an outage keep eggs in the coolest place possible and use oldest first.
  • Freeze extra eggs: crack, beat lightly, freeze in portions (ice cube trays). Don't freeze in the shell.
  • Traditional preservation (like "water-glassing" in pickling lime) is popular but not tested/approved by the USDA — use at your own risk; discard any egg that smells off or floats suspiciously.
  • Cook eggs fully for the kids, Grandma and Grandpa, and Mom.

Flock health and bird flu

  • Avian influenza (bird flu) has hit Colorado poultry and dairies, including in nearby Weld County. Keep wild birds out of feed and water, don't touch sick or dead wild birds, wash hands, use dedicated boots for the coop.
  • Sudden deaths, swollen heads, purple combs, or a big drop in eating/laying: don't eat those birds or eggs; report to the Colorado Department of Agriculture State Veterinarian's office.
  • If you butcher an old hen for soup: only healthy birds, cook thoroughly.

Do it now

  • Stockpile 3+ months of layer feed (sealed metal cans) + oyster shell.
  • Line up a local farmer/elevator for bulk wheat, corn, or milo.
  • Coop light on a timer; heated waterer (+ manual backup).
  • Start a mealworm bin now; plan BSFL bin for next summer.
  • Grow extra squash/pumpkins/beets next summer for winter greens.
  • Get a rooster or plan spring chicks to keep young layers coming.
  • Predator-proof the coop with hardware cloth.

Rabbits & Feeding Eight People

Rabbits are one of the best survival meat animals: quiet, small, fast-breeding, and they turn hay and greens into meat. But you cannot live on rabbit alone — the meat is very lean. This chapter covers raising, butchering, storing, and how rabbit fits into a full diet for 8 people.

A breeding plan

  • Keep a few good does (females) and 1–2 bucks (males), housed separately.
  • Always take the doe to the buck's cage (does defend their space).
  • Put a nest box with straw/hay in at day 27–28. The doe pulls fur to line it.
  • A moderate, sustainable plan: rebreed the doe when kits are about 4–6 weeks old, giving about 4–5 litters a year per doe. Rest does that look thin.
  • Keep records: doe, buck, breeding date, kindling date, number of kits, weaned, butchered.
  • Avoid inbreeding: bring in an unrelated buck every year or two (trade with neighbors).

Rough output: 4 does × 4–5 litters × ~6 kits raised ≈ about 100 fryers a year — roughly one rabbit every 3–4 days. Great protein, but not enough calories alone for 8 people (see math below).

Feeding rabbits — especially in winter

  • Unlimited grass hay (or grass/alfalfa mix) is the base of the diet. Stock hay before winter — it's cheap here in fall and scarce after a bad winter. Track how fast we use it.
  • Pellets if available (stockpile in sealed cans). If pellets run out: hay + oats/barley (small amounts) + greens.
  • Safe greens and extras: dandelions, plantain, clover, grass, carrot tops, kale, cabbage (small amounts), sunflower heads, sprouted grain fodder, tree branches (apple, willow — rabbits love to chew).
  • Avoid: moldy hay, lawn clippings that have fermented, potato leaves, rhubarb, nightshade, avocado, onions, ice-cold or spoiled food, and any meat.
  • Water is the #1 winter problem. Bottles freeze and crack — use heavy crocks and swap with warm water 2–3 times a day. Nursing does drink a lot.

Winter and summer care

  • Rabbits handle cold well if dry and out of the wind. Wrap hutches on the north/west, add a roof overhang, and give extra hay to burrow into.
  • Winter kits are at risk of freezing. Give a deep, well-bedded nest box; move the nest box (or the doe's cage) to a sheltered spot like a garage or barn in a cold snap. Or plan breeding so most kits are born in milder months.
  • Heat is the bigger killer — rabbits suffer above about 85°F. In summer: shade, airflow, frozen water bottles to lean against.

Housing tip

Consider the Raken house (Chapter 5): rabbit cages hung above the chicken floor. The rabbits' manure feeds the deep bedding the hens scratch for bugs, and one building is easier to keep out of the wind and keep water thawed.

Butchering (humane and clean)

  1. Withhold feed about 12 hours before (keep water).
  2. Dispatch quickly and humanely (learn from an experienced person or a CSU Extension class — practice now).
  3. Hang, skin, and gut on a clean surface. Keep the bladder and intestines intact.
  4. Check the liver: it should be smooth and dark red. White spots may mean parasites/disease — throw out the liver; if the whole carcass looks unhealthy, discard it.
  5. Wear gloves. Rabbits can carry tularemia, a bacterial disease. Domestic rabbits are low risk; wild rabbits acting sick or sluggish — don't handle or eat. (CDC)
  6. Chill the meat quickly (cold water bath or fridge).
  7. Organs and scraps → cook for the chickens (Chapter 5). Hides can be tanned; manure goes to the garden (rabbit manure can go straight on beds).

Storing rabbit meat

  • Fridge: cook within 1–2 days.
  • Freezer: months. Wrap tightly to prevent freezer burn.
  • Winter: a cold snap is a natural freezer — but meat outside must be protected from animals and kept solidly frozen, not thawing in sun.
  • Canning: meat must be pressure-canned using a tested USDA/Extension recipe. Never water-bath can meat — botulism risk.
  • Jerky: USDA recommends heating meat to 160°F before or after drying for safety.
  • Cooking: cook rabbit thoroughly — to 160°F internal temperature. (USDA)

⚠ "Rabbit starvation" (protein poisoning)

Rabbit meat is very lean — mostly protein, little fat. People who eat only lean meat for days to weeks get sick: nausea, diarrhea, weakness, and constant hunger, and can actually starve while eating plenty. Your body can only safely handle a limited share of calories from protein; the rest must come from fat and carbohydrates.

Always pair rabbit with:

  • Fat: cooking oil, lard, butter/ghee, bacon grease, peanut butter, nuts, egg yolks.
  • Carbs: rice, wheat/flour, oats, potatoes, beans, corn, squash.
  • Example meal: rabbit stew with potatoes, beans, and a big spoon of lard or oil; eggs on the side.

Food Storage, Gardening & Foraging on the Plains

Chapter 6 showed that eggs and rabbits supply protein, but stored staples must supply most of our calories. This chapter covers what to store, how to cook without power, how to garden in our short, windy, hail-prone season, and what we can realistically find, hunt, or fish around Wiggins.

Storage principles

  1. Store what we already eat, and eat what we store (rotate: new in back, old in front).
  2. Cool, dark, dry, and rodent-proof. Basement shelves are good. Off the floor.
  3. Bulk grains and beans: food-grade 5-gallon buckets with Mylar bags and oxygen absorbers, or sealed metal cans. Stored this way, dry white rice, wheat, and dry beans keep for many years (Extension guidance).
  4. Write the date on everything.
  5. Manual can opener (two!).

How much — the LDS (Latter-day Saints) method

The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints has taught home food storage for generations, and its method is simple and well tested:

  1. Start with one week of the food you normally eat (what you buy for a day × 7).
  2. Grow it to a three-month supply of normal foods, rotated.
  3. Then build a longer-term supply of basics that keep you alive: wheat, white rice, and beans. Properly packaged and kept cool (75°F or below) and dry, these can last 30 years or more. Cooking oil needs rotating every 1–2 years.

Their one-year "basics" per adult (Church guidance): 400 lb grains, 60 lb legumes, 16 lb powdered milk, 10 qt cooking oil, 60 lb sugar or honey, 8 lb salt, plus 2 weeks of water. Children get a share: 3 and under 50%, 4–6 70%, 7–10 90%, and they suggest using next year's age for growing kids. Nursing babies share Mom's portion.

Cooking without power

  • Wood stove top (once reconnected) — great for pots of beans, stew, and heating water.

  • In winter, move food to coolers outside in the shade (protected from animals) when it is below freezing.
  • "When in doubt, throw it out" — especially meat, dairy, eggs, leftovers above 40°F for more than 2 hours.

Gardening on the eastern plains

Our challenges: short season, late spring and early fall frosts, hail, wind, alkaline soil, and limited water.

  • Frost dates: around Wiggins the average last spring frost is roughly mid-May and the first fall frost roughly late September to early October. Check CSU Extension / Colorado Climate Center for exact local dates.
  • Best calorie crops for us: potatoes, winter squash and pumpkins (store all winter — and feed chickens), dry beans, corn, sunflowers (seeds for people, chickens, and rabbits).
  • Fast greens: lettuce, spinach, kale, radishes, peas (cool season — plant early spring and again late summer).
  • Protect from hail and wind: hail cloth or row covers on hoops, windbreak fences, raised beds.
  • Season extenders: cold frames, low tunnels, a small hoop house — can stretch greens well into fall.
  • Water: drip irrigation and thick mulch (straw). Colorado law lets homeowners collect rooftop rainwater in up to two rain barrels totaling 110 gallons, for outdoor use. Domestic well permits may limit outdoor watering — check ours.
  • Rabbit and chicken manure = free fertilizer (compost chicken manure first; rabbit manure can go on directly).
  • Save seeds from open-pollinated (heirloom) varieties. Keep a stash of seeds in a cool dry place — buy extra now.
  • Root cellar / basement storage: potatoes, squash, carrots, onions, and cabbage keep for months in a cool, dark, slightly humid space.

Hypothermia (body too cold) (CDC, Wilderness Medical Society)

In a long outage, hypothermia can happen indoors — especially to the newborn, toddlers, and Grandma and Grandpa.

Signs — adults: shivering, clumsy hands, stumbling, slurred speech, confusion, drowsiness. Shivering that STOPS while still cold is a bad sign. Infants: bright red, cold skin and very low energy; weak cry; poor feeding.

What to do (no help coming):

  1. Move them to the warmest room. Get them off the floor and out of wind.
  2. Remove wet clothes; dry them.
  3. Wrap like a burrito: sleeping bag or blankets, plus a vapor barrier (plastic sheet, trash bags, or emergency blanket) outside the insulation, and cover the head and neck.
  4. Heat to the chest, armpits, and back — warm (not hot) water bottles wrapped in cloth, or skin-to-skin with a warm person inside the wrap. For a baby, skin-to-skin on Mom or Dad's chest, covered, is the best warmer there is.
  5. If awake and able to swallow: warm, sweet drinks and food (sugar fuels shivering). No alcohol.
  6. Handle severe hypothermia gently — rough movement can upset the heart. Keep them lying flat.
  7. Keep checking breathing. Very cold people can have a slow, faint pulse — check for a full minute before deciding there is none.

Prevention is everything: one heated room, dry layers, hats, eat and drink regularly, check the babies and elders every hour at night.

Frostbite (CDC, Wilderness Medical Society)

Signs: skin turns red, then white or grayish-yellow, hard or waxy, and numb. Fingers, toes, ears, nose, cheeks.

What to do (no help coming):

  1. Treat hypothermia first.
  2. Don't rewarm if it could freeze again (e.g., you still have to walk home in the storm). Refreezing does far more damage. Walk on frozen feet if you must to reach shelter — then rewarm once safe.
  3. Rewarm fast in warm water — about 99–102°F (comfortably warm to an elbow; use a thermometer if you have one) for 15–30 minutes, until the skin is soft and color returns. Add warm water as it cools. It will hurt a lot — give ibuprofen (it also helps the tissue) and acetaminophen per Chapter 9 doses.
  4. Never rub with snow or hands, never use a stove, fire, or heating pad — numb skin burns.
  5. After thawing: don't pop clear blisters; put aloe vera gel on, wrap loosely with dry gauze between fingers and toes, elevate, keep it from refreezing.
  6. Blood-filled blisters or skin that stays black = deep injury. Keep it clean and dry; dead tissue often separates on its own over weeks. Watch daily for infection (Chapter 9). Don't try to cut away blackened tissue — even surgeons wait.

Carbon monoxide poisoning (CDC)

The most likely way a grid-down winter kills a whole family. Battery CO alarms in every sleeping area are not optional.

Signs: headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea or vomiting, confusion, sleepiness. Several people (or pets) sick at once indoors = think CO first.

What to do: everyone out into fresh air immediately, open doors and windows, shut off the source (generator, heater, stove, blocked chimney). Keep the sick outside (bundled) or in a ventilated room until the alarm reads zero. Headache may last hours. Fresh air is the only home treatment — someone unconscious or confused after CO has a poor outlook without hospital oxygen, which is why prevention is the real answer.

Choking and CPR — what can still save a life

  • Choking — infants under 1: 5 back blows (baby face-down on your forearm), then 5 chest thrusts with two fingers — repeat until it comes out or baby goes limp, then start infant CPR. Children and adults: abdominal thrusts (Heimlich).
  • CPR without hospitals — honest truth: for an adult whose heart has stopped from a heart attack, CPR alone (no defibrillator or hospital) rarely brings them back. But CPR with rescue breaths does save children and people who stopped breathing from choking, drowning, smoke, or CO — because their heart is often still fine. Learn it.

Strokes and heart attacks (Grandma and Grandpa)

  • Heart attack signs: chest pressure or pain spreading to arm, jaw, or back, sweating, short of breath, nausea. Without services: have them rest sitting up, keep them calm and warm, and if they are not allergic and not on blood thinners, chew one regular (325 mg) aspirin (American Heart Association advice for suspected heart attack). Ask their doctor now whether that's right for them.
  • Stroke signs (BE FAST): Balance, Eyes, Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech trouble, Time. Without services: lay them on their side if drowsy, nothing by mouth until they can swallow safely, prevent falls, keep them warm and comfortable. Recovery care is mostly nursing care at home — feeding, turning, toileting.
  • Prevention now: keep their blood pressure, heart, and diabetes medicines filled with 90-day supplies, and get a home blood-pressure cuff.

Do it now

  • CPR/First Aid with infant & child (all four adults); Stop the Bleed; a WFA or WFR course for Daniel and/or wife.
  • Battery CO alarms in every sleeping area, both houses.
  • Sleeping bags, wool blankets, emergency blankets, hand warmers.
  • A cooking thermometer that reads water temperature (frostbite rewarming).
  • Ask Grandma and Grandpa's doctor about 90-day supplies and the aspirin question.
  • Build the home medical kit in Chapter 9.

Chapter 9

Home Medicine When No Help Is Coming

This chapter assumes the worst: no 911, no ER, no pharmacy, no doctor — for weeks or longer. It is written so that a careful parent can handle the injuries and illnesses a farm family is most likely to face, using what we stock now.

If services ARE running: use them — 911, St. Elizabeth Hospital ER, 1000 Lincoln St, Fort Morgan (970-867-3391, entrance on Grant St), Poison Control 1-800-222-1222. Everything below is for when that isn't possible.

Part 1 — Mindset and triage

Be honest about what home care can and can't do

Most injuries and illnesses get better with good home care: cuts and scrapes, sprains, most simple broken bones, minor burns, colds, flu, stomach bugs, ear infections, most fevers in children over 3 months. Good nursing — fluids, warmth, cleanliness, rest, food — is what saves most lives. That was true for our great-grandparents, and it is true now.

Some things are very likely to be fatal without a hospital, and no book can change that: serious internal bleeding (e.g., from a crushed belly or a kicked chest), severe head injuries with worsening drowsiness, appendicitis that ruptures, large deep burns (more than about a quarter of the body), sepsis (blood infection) without antibiotics, open breaks of the thigh, severe allergic reactions without epinephrine, a baby born very early or a complicated birth, and a major heart attack or stroke. With these, we still do everything we can — comfort, warmth, fluids, cleanliness, and love — and some people do survive. Knowing this ahead of time helps us prevent these injuries (tractor, livestock, ladder, stove, and gun safety) and plan honestly.

The order of priorities (ABC + bleeding first)

  1. Danger: make the scene safe (fire, CO, animals, machinery, ice).
  2. Massive bleeding: stop it first — a person can bleed to death in minutes (Part 2).
  3. Airway: can they breathe? Unconscious and breathing → roll onto their side (recovery position) so vomit drains.
  4. Breathing: count breaths; look for blue lips, struggling.
  5. Circulation: pulse, skin color and temperature; treat for shock — lay flat, keep warm.
  6. Everything else: broken bones, burns, cleaning wounds, pain.

Set up a sick room

  • One room, away from the babies' sleeping area, near the bucket toilet if possible.
  • A bed you can reach from both sides, a bucket for vomit, a trash bag for dressings, a handwashing station (soap, water jug with a spout, basin), a bleach spray bottle.
  • One caregiver for contagious illness when possible; that person washes hands and changes outer clothes before handling the babies.
  • Good light (headlamp), a chair, a clock, a notebook.

Keep records — they are your "lab tests"

Without a doctor, trends tell you if someone is getting better or worse. Write every few hours:

Part 2 — Bleeding control (Stop the Bleed, Wilderness Medical Society, military TCCC)

Step 1: Direct pressure — hard and long

Put gauze or clean cloth on the wound and press hard with both hands, straight down, for at least 10 minutes (count it on a clock). Don't peek. If it soaks through, add more on top and press harder.

Step 2: Pack deep wounds

For a deep wound that keeps bleeding — especially in the armpit, groin, or neck where a tourniquet can't go:

  1. Find where the blood is coming from.
  2. Stuff gauze into the wound — all the way to the bottom — pushing it in with your fingers, filling the hole completely. Hemostatic gauze (QuikClot, Celox) works best; plain gauze or clean cloth works too.
  3. Press on top for at least 3–5 minutes (hemostatic gauze needs at least 5).
  4. Wrap tightly with a pressure bandage (an "Israeli" emergency bandage or an elastic wrap) to hold it.

Step 3: Tourniquet — arms and legs

For life-threatening bleeding from an arm or leg (spurting, pooling, soaking through), use a tourniquet right away — don't wait.

  • Use a real commercial tourniquet (CAT or SOF-T from a trusted seller — knock-offs break). An improvised one needs a strap at least 1½ inches wide and a windlass (stick) to twist it tight — a belt alone won't work. (WMS)
  • Place it 2–3 inches above the wound (not on a joint), "high and tight" if unsure.
  • Tighten until the bleeding stops and the pulse below it disappears. It will hurt badly — that's normal. A loose tourniquet makes bleeding worse.
  • Write the time on it or on the skin.
  • Time limits (no hospital coming): a tourniquet can stay on for up to 2 hours with little risk. Within that time, once the person is stable, pack and pressure-wrap the wound, then slowly loosen the tourniquet while watching. If bleeding stays controlled, leave it loose (in place, ready). If bleeding returns, re-tighten. Don't loosen it "every so often" to let blood flow — that's an old, harmful myth. (WMS) Honest truth: with no surgery available, a tourniquet that must stay on for many hours usually means losing the limb — but it saves the life.

Shock

After big blood loss: pale, cold, sweaty, fast weak pulse, fast breathing, confused, thirsty. Lay them flat, keep them warm (cold makes bleeding worse), raise legs if no leg injury, and if awake and not vomiting, give small sips of oral rehydration solution. Keep checking.

Part 3 — Wound care without a doctor

1. Clean — the step that prevents most infections

  • Wash your hands, wear gloves.
  • Irrigate with at least 1 liter (about a quart) of drinkable water — more for big or dirty wounds. Clean drinking water works as well as sterile saline. (WMS)
  • Pressure helps: squirt with a large syringe (20–60 mL) — ideally with an 18–19-gauge irrigation tip or catheter — or poke a pin-hole in a clean zip bag and squeeze. Hold the wound open and flush from the inside out.
  • To make saline (gentler, if you prefer): boil water 1 minute, then add about 2 level teaspoons of table salt per liter (quart) and let cool, covered. Use within 24 hours.
  • Animal bites (rabies risk): scrub with soap and water for 15 minutes, then rinse with povidone-iodine (Betadine) diluted to the color of weak tea. (WMS / WHO rabies guidance)
  • Don't keep pouring hydrogen peroxide or rubbing alcohol into wounds — they damage tissue.

2. Debride — remove dirt and dead tissue

  • Pick out dirt, gravel, wood, and hair with tweezers boiled for 10 minutes or wiped with alcohol.
  • Trim only obviously dead, loose flaps of skin or tissue with clean scissors. Don't cut into healthy (bleeding, pink) tissue.
  • Ground-in dirt left in a wound causes infection and permanent "tattoo" marks — scrub gently with a soft brush and soapy water.

3. Close it — or leave it open?

This is the most important decision in home wound care. Closing a dirty wound seals germs inside, and that can kill.

OK to close (after thorough cleaning): clean, straight cuts, less than about 12 hours old (face up to 24), not from bites, not deep punctures, not crushed.

  • Wound-closure strips / butterfly strips: best home choice. Dry the skin (tincture of benzoin helps them stick), lay strips across the cut ⅛ inch apart, pulling edges together gently.
  • Skin glue (medical tissue adhesive like Dermabond, or "liquid bandage"): small, clean, low-tension cuts. Hold edges together, paint glue on top — not inside the cut.
  • Hair apposition (scalp cuts): twist a few hairs from each side together across the cut and dab with glue.

Leave OPEN: animal and human bites, punctures, dirty or crushed wounds, wounds older than ~12 hours, deep wounds you can't clean to the bottom, and any wound that looks infected. Pack lightly with moist gauze, cover, and change daily. These heal from the bottom up — slower, more scar, but much safer.

Delayed closure: a wound left open can sometimes be closed with strips at day 3–5 if it is clean, pink, and has no sign of infection. (WMS)

Suturing (stitches) — the honest version

Stitching a wound closes in any germs left behind, and badly placed stitches can cause deep infection. Most survival-medicine teachers say: if in doubt, don't suture. Strips and glue handle most cuts; open healing handles the rest. If someone in the family wants this skill:

  • Take a class (some WFR courses and survival-medicine workshops teach it) and practice on pig's feet or pork belly until it's easy.
  • Only on clean, fresh, simple wounds, after excellent irrigation; never bites, punctures, or dirty wounds.
  • Stitches come out in about 5 days on the face, 7–10 on the body, 10–14 over joints.

4. Dress it and keep it clean

  • A thin layer of petroleum jelly, antibiotic ointment, or medical-grade honey, then a non-stick pad, then gauze or a wrap.
  • Change once a day (or if wet or dirty). Look, smell, and compare to yesterday — write it in the log.
  • Keep flies and dirt off — cover fully, especially in summer around chickens, rabbits, and manure. Fly eggs (maggots) in a wound need flushing out.
  • Elevate swollen arms and legs above the heart.
  • Tetanus: get everyone's shots up to date now (adults every 10 years; a booster after 5 years for dirty wounds — CDC). With no services, there is no tetanus shot after an injury — only prevention works.

Part 4 — Infection when there are no antibiotics

Know the stages

Stage What you see What to do
Normal healing a little pink at the edges, mild soreness, clear fluid the first 1–2 days keep clean and covered
Local infection spreading redness, swelling, warmth, worsening pain after day 2, pus open it up, soaks, honey, elevate, rest (below)
Abscess a tender, swollen, soft, fluctuant ("squishy") lump of pus warm soaks; may need draining (below)
Spreading infection (cellulitis) redness growing — mark its edge with a pen and check every few hours; red streaks up the limb serious — all of the below, plus rest and fluids; antibiotics if you have a prescribed supply
Sepsis (blood infection) fever OR low temperature, fast pulse, fast breathing, confusion, mottled or gray skin, very little urine, feels "like dying" life-threatening. Fluids, warmth, rest, fever medicine. Without antibiotics, many people do not survive.

What we can do at home

  • Open and drain: if a closed wound becomes infected, remove the strips or stitches, let it drain, irrigate, and leave it open.
  • Warm soaks or compresses — 15–20 minutes, 4 times a day — bring blood flow and help pus come to a head.
  • Honey dressings (medical-grade) on infected open wounds; change daily.
  • Elevate and rest the part; splint an infected hand or foot so it doesn't move.
  • Fluids, food, sleep. The body does the real fighting.
  • Draining an abscess (only if it is clearly soft and pointing near the surface, not on the face, neck, hand, or near big blood vessels): clean the skin, use a sterile blade or needle (boiled 10 minutes) to make one small cut at the softest, most-raised point, let the pus drain, gently press around (not on) it, irrigate, and pack loosely with a strip of moist gauze, changing daily. Don't cut a hard, red lump — keep up warm soaks until it softens. (Where There Is No Doctor)

Antibiotics — plan NOW, not later

  • Talk to our family doctor now about a prescribed emergency supply of antibiotics for a family with young children far from care (some doctors and telehealth services that make emergency kits will do this). Ask which drugs, which child-safe forms (liquids need water and spoil — ask about chewables or powders), doses by weight, what each is for, and allergies. Write it all down and keep the paper with the medicine.
  • Fish and animal antibiotics are a real temptation — and a real danger. They aren't made or inspected for people, the label may not match what's inside, and using the wrong drug, wrong dose, or wrong length can fail to cure the infection, breed resistant germs, or cause a dangerous allergic reaction with no hospital to treat it. This book will not give doses for them. Get a real prescription while you can.
  • Allergy check: know who in the family is allergic to penicillin or sulfa. Stock diphenhydramine (Benadryl) — and ask the doctor about an epinephrine auto-injector if anyone has had a severe reaction.

Part 5 — Fever and sickness at home

Fluids are the #1 lifesaver

More children around the world die from dehydration from diarrhea and vomiting than from the germs themselves. Oral rehydration solution (ORS) saves lives. (WHO)

Store-bought ORS packets (WHO formula) or Pedialyte are best — stock plenty. If you run out:

Home ORS: 1 liter (about 1 quart) of clean, boiled-and-cooled water + 6 level teaspoons sugar + ½ level teaspoon salt. Stir. Use within 24 hours. (WHO / Rehydration Project)

  • Measure exactly — too much salt is dangerous for children; a little too dilute is harmless.
  • How much: after each watery stool, under 2 years ¼–½ cup; 2 and older ½–1 cup; adults as much as they want. (WHO)
  • Vomiting: give 1 teaspoon (5 mL) every 1–2 minutes with a spoon or syringe. That small amount usually stays down and adds up to about a cup an hour. After 4 hours without vomiting, give more.
  • Babies: keep breastfeeding — more often, not less. Breast milk is the best rehydration a baby can get. Formula-fed baby: keep formula at normal strength; offer ORS between feeds if the baby is dehydrated (WHO-type ORS packets are made for all ages; homemade ORS only if nothing else, in small amounts).
  • Older kids and adults: keep eating bland foods (rice, crackers, bananas, soups, eggs) once they're hungry. Feeding during diarrhea helps recovery.

Fever

Fever is the body fighting infection. In children over 3 months it is usually not dangerous by itself — how the child acts and drinks matters more than the number. (AAP)

  • Track it on the log: temperature, time, medicine, drinking, behavior.
  • Fluids, light clothing, rest. Lukewarm sponging if uncomfortable — never cold water, ice, or rubbing alcohol.
  • Medicine is for comfort (doses below). It lets a child drink and sleep.
  • Warning signs (worse, not better): hard to wake or very floppy; stiff neck; purple/red rash that doesn't fade when you press a glass on it; struggling to breathe; refusing all fluids; seizure; fever above 104°F that won't come down; fever over 3 days. With no help available: all of the care in this section, constantly, plus antibiotics if you have a prescribed plan for it.

A fever in our newborn (under 3 months)

In normal times, any temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher in a baby under 3 months means the ER immediately — young babies can get blood, urine, or brain infections that look mild at first. (AAP) With no help coming, here's what we can still do:

  1. Recheck the temperature (rectal is most accurate). Is the baby overdressed or next to the stove? Unbundle, recheck in 15–20 minutes.
  2. Keep breastfeeding — often. Feeding is the baby's lifeline. If too weak to nurse, give expressed breast milk by spoon, cup, or syringe in small amounts often.
  3. Count wet diapers and watch the soft spot.
  4. Keep the baby at a comfortable temperature — not bundled hot, not chilled.
  5. Acetaminophen only if a doctor gave you a dose for this baby's weight in advance (ask the pediatrician now what to do in this exact situation and write it here: ______________).
  6. Watch for sepsis signs: won't feed, very sleepy or limp, grunting or fast breathing, pale, mottled, or blue color, very low temperature (below 97°F), bulging soft spot, seizures.
  7. No ibuprofen, aspirin, honey, herbal teas, or water for a newborn.

Honest truth: a very sick young baby can die without hospital care even with perfect home care. That's why breastfeeding, handwashing, keeping sick people away from the baby, and vaccines on schedule now matter so much.

Vomiting and diarrhea — and stopping it from spreading

  • ORS as above; don't give anti-diarrhea medicine (loperamide/Imodium) to young children (not under 2; label says 6 and up) — adults only, and not with bloody diarrhea or high fever.
  • Blood in stool with fever = dysentery. Fluids, food, rest; this is one of the main reasons to have a prescribed antibiotic plan.
  • Stomach bugs (like norovirus) tear through a household. Hand sanitizer doesn't kill norovirus well — soap and running water does. Clean vomit and diarrhea with bleach solution: 5–25 tablespoons of household bleach per gallon of water (stronger for heavy soiling) and let it sit 5 minutes. (CDC) Wash soiled laundry hot; wear gloves.
  • The sick person doesn't cook. Separate towels and cups. Keep them away from the babies.

Coughs, colds, and breathing trouble

  • Most coughs and colds clear in 1–2 weeks. Rest, fluids, humidified air (a pot of water near — not on — the stove, or a steamy bathroom), saline nose drops and a bulb syringe for babies (they must breathe through their noses to nurse).
  • Honey for cough in kids over 1 year (½–1 teaspoon) works about as well as cough syrup. (AAP) No cough or cold medicines under 4.
  • Croup (barking, seal-like cough in toddlers, often at night): sit them up, keep calm, try cool night air (bundle up by an open door or window for a few minutes) or a steamy bathroom.
  • Danger signs (pneumonia or worse): fast breathing (above the normal range table), skin pulling in between ribs or at the neck, nostrils flaring, grunting, blue or gray lips, oximeter below ~92%, too breathless to talk or feed. With no help: keep them sitting up, fluids in small sips, fever control, rest, warmth, oximeter checks, and antibiotics if prescribed for this. These are the illnesses where a home pulse oximeter earns its price.

Prevent the illness in the first place

  • Handwashing with soap before food and after the toilet, diapers, and animals — the single most effective "medicine" in this book.
  • Safe water (Chapter 2), safe toilets away from water and food, flies kept off food.
  • Quarantine new illness: if neighbors are sick, limit contact for a few days.
  • Vaccines on schedule now — whooping cough, measles, and flu kill babies when hospitals aren't there.

Part 6 — Broken bones, sprains, and dislocations

Sprains and strains

Rest, ice or cold (snow in a bag — 20 minutes on, wrapped in cloth), compression wrap, elevation. Ibuprofen helps. Start gentle movement after 2–3 days. A sprain that can't take any weight after a few days may be a break — splint it.

Broken bones (fractures)

Signs: deformity, severe pain at one spot, swelling, can't use it, grinding.

  1. Check circulation, feeling, and movement below the injury (pulse, warm pink fingers or toes, can they feel your touch?).
  2. Splint it so the joints above and below the break can't move. Use a SAM splint, boards, rolled magazines, or cardboard, padded well, tied with cloth or elastic wrap — snug, not tight. Fingers: tape to the next finger ("buddy taping"). Arm: sling and swathe to the body.
  3. Badly bent limb with no pulse beyond it: gently pull in line with the bone (steady traction along its length) to bring it back to roughly straight, then splint. Stop if pain becomes extreme or you feel resistance. (Wilderness First Responder teaching)
  4. Recheck circulation every hour — loosen if fingers/toes go cold, pale, numb, or blue.
  5. Healing time: most simple breaks need about 6 weeks in a splint (less in young kids, more in older adults). Keep elevated the first days.

Open fracture (bone through skin): very dangerous without surgery and antibiotics. Control bleeding, irrigate with lots of clean water, cover with moist sterile gauze, splint, and watch for infection constantly. Use a prescribed antibiotic plan if you have one.

Thigh (femur) or pelvis fractures can bleed internally and are often fatal without a hospital. Keep the person flat and still, warm, splint both legs together, pain control, fluids.

Neck or back injury (fall from a hayloft, thrown from a horse, rollover): if neck pain, numbness, tingling, or weakness — keep the head and body still and in line, roll as a unit with helpers. Many neck injuries heal with rest and a padded collar (towel roll), but paralysis cannot be reversed at home.

Dislocations

  • Fingers and kneecaps often go back with gentle, steady straightening (a kneecap usually slides back when the leg is slowly straightened). Splint afterward.
  • Shoulders can sometimes be put back by trained people with slow, gentle techniques — learn them in a WFR course; don't yank. If it can't be put back, sling it in the most comfortable position.
  • Elbows, hips, and anything with a possible fracture: don't try — splint as found.

Part 7 — Burns over the days that follow

  1. Cool running water for 20 minutes — first, always. No ice, butter, or oil.
  2. Estimate size: the person's palm (with fingers) ≈ 1% of their body. Over about 10% in a child or 15% in an adult loses enough fluid to be dangerous — push ORS hard (small amounts very often) and watch urine.
  3. Clean gently each day with clean water and mild soap. Leave blisters intact; trim away blisters that have burst.
  4. Dress: thin layer of medical-grade honey (good evidence for partial-thickness burns — Cochrane), silver sulfadiazine if prescribed, or petroleum jelly, then non-stick pads and loose gauze. Change daily. Separate burned fingers with gauze.
  5. Keep moving burned joints gently a few times a day so they don't heal stiff; elevate swollen limbs.
  6. Pain: ibuprofen and acetaminophen together (doses above).
  7. Watch for infection (Part 4) — the main killer of burn patients after the first days.
  8. Deep burns (white, leathery, painless, or charred) heal very slowly from the edges and scar; large ones are often fatal without a hospital. Keep clean, covered, fed, hydrated, and warm.

Prevention: stove gates, pot handles turned in, hot liquids out of kids' reach, no kids near the wood stove or burn barrel.

Part 8 — Toothaches and dental emergencies (Where There Is No Dentist; American Dental Association)

  • Pain: for adults, the ADA recommends ibuprofen plus acetaminophen together as the best non-opioid dental pain relief. Kids: weight-based doses above.
  • Clove oil (eugenol) on a cotton bit, held on the tooth, numbs pain (traditional, with some evidence). Don't swallow it, and don't use on young children.
  • Lost filling or broken tooth: temporary filling material (pharmacy dental kits like Dentemp) covers it.
  • Warm salt-water rinses (½ tsp salt in a cup of warm water) several times a day for sore gums and infections.
  • Knocked-out adult tooth: hold by the crown (not the root), rinse briefly, push back into the socket within minutes if possible, bite on gauze; or store in milk. Don't replant baby teeth.
  • Danger: a tooth infection with swelling spreading to the eye, jaw, or neck, trouble swallowing or breathing, or high fever can kill. Warm rinses, pain control, and a prescribed antibiotic plan; an abscess pointing in the gum can be drained like Part 4.
  • Prevention now: dental checkups for everyone, fix problem teeth while dentists exist, brush and floss — and stock toothbrushes, floss, and fluoride toothpaste.

Part 9 — Herbs and kitchen remedies: what the evidence says

With pharmacies closed, people turn to plants. Some help with comfort; none replace fluids, wound cleaning, or antibiotics. Strong = good human studies; Some = a few studies or well-documented traditional use; Weak = mostly tradition or lab work.

Safety first:

  • Identify plants with 100% certainty — learn them from CSU Extension or an experienced local now. Deadly look-alikes grow by the South Platte (water hemlock, poison hemlock — Chapter 7).
  • Breastfeeding Mom and the babies: avoid herbal medicines beyond normal food amounts unless a doctor okays them; little is known about safety for infants. (NCCIH)
  • Ragweed/daisy family allergy: avoid yarrow, chamomile, and echinacea.
  • Herbs can interact with medicines (especially blood thinners).

Part 10 — Dangers of our area, with no help coming

Rattlesnake bites

Prairie rattlesnakes live in the grasslands, rocky areas, and outbuildings of eastern Colorado, mostly April–October. (Colorado Parks and Wildlife)

The honest picture: antivenom (only in hospitals) is the real treatment. Without it, most adults bitten by a prairie rattlesnake survive, but bites can cause severe swelling, tissue death, bleeding problems, and lasting damage, and small children are at much greater risk because they get the same venom in a smaller body. Some bites are "dry" (little or no venom).

What to do:

  1. Move away from the snake. Don't try to catch or kill it.
  2. Remove rings, watches, boots, tight clothes from the bitten limb immediately.
  3. Keep the person calm, lying down, and still; keep the limb still (splint it) at about heart level. (Wilderness Medical Society)
  4. Mark the edge of swelling with a pen and the time, every 30 minutes.
  5. Fluids by mouth if not vomiting; acetaminophen for pain — NOT ibuprofen or aspirin (they can worsen bleeding).
  6. Watch for shock (Part 2) and bleeding from gums, nose, or old cuts; treat shock.
  7. Over days: keep the limb elevated after the first hours, clean any blisters, and watch for infection.

Do NOT: cut the bite, suck out venom (no device works), use a tourniquet or tight wrap, put ice on it, give alcohol, or use electric shock. (WMS, CDC)

Prevention is the real treatment: boots and long pants, look before reaching into woodpiles, hay, and under coops and hutches, mow around buildings, teach kids to freeze and back away.

Frostbite and hypothermia

See Chapter 8 — rewarm frostbite in warm water (about 99–102°F) only when it won't refreeze; warm the core first for hypothermia.

Heat illness

  • Heat exhaustion (heavy sweating, weak, dizzy, headache, nausea): shade, lie down, loosen clothes, wet cloths, sip ORS.
  • Heat stroke (hot skin, confusion, collapse, may stop sweating) is deadly: cool them as fast as possible — soak with water and fan hard, or put them in a stock tank or tub of cool water (stay with them and keep the head up), ice or cold packs at neck, armpits, groin. Keep cooling until they're alert. (CDC, WMS)
  • Babies, toddlers, and Grandma and Grandpa overheat fastest.

Dehydration — dry air and altitude

Our air is very dry and we're at about 4,500 feet — you lose water just breathing, working outside, or sitting by the wood stove. Drink before you're thirsty; check that urine is pale. Nursing Mom needs extra.

Hantavirus — from mouse dust

A rare but often deadly lung infection from deer mice in barns, sheds, coops, feed rooms, and vehicles. Caught by breathing dust from droppings, urine, and nests. Colorado has cases most years. There is no home cure — prevention is everything. (CDC, CDPHE)

Safe cleanup (CDC): open doors and windows 30 minutes first and leave; gloves on; do NOT sweep, vacuum, or leaf-blow; spray droppings and nests until soaking wet with 1½ cups bleach per gallon of water (fresh); soak 5 minutes; wipe up with paper towels, bag, mop with bleach solution; wash gloves, then hands. A well-fitted N95 mask adds protection.

Symptoms (1–8 weeks later): fever, aching thighs, hips, and back, fatigue, then cough and shortness of breath. With no hospital: rest, fluids, fever control, sitting up, oxygen checks — and honestly, severe cases are often fatal even in hospitals.

Prevention: feed in metal cans with lids; seal gaps; trap mice; keep spilled feed cleaned up.

Tularemia — from rabbits

"Rabbit fever," a bacterial infection from rabbits and rodents, tick and deer fly bites, or dust (mowing over a dead rabbit). Seen in Colorado most years. (CDC, CDPHE)

  • Wear gloves when butchering (Chapter 6); don't butcher a rabbit that seemed sick; never handle wild rabbits found dead bare-handed. Cook rabbit to 160°F.
  • Repellent and tick checks.
  • Symptoms: sudden high fever, chills, swollen lymph nodes, a sore where it entered. It is treatable with the right antibiotics — another reason for a prescribed plan. Without them: rest, fluids, fever care.

Also in our area

  • West Nile virus (mosquitoes by the river and ditches): repellent at dawn and dusk, dump standing water. Most cases are mild; older adults are at highest risk.
  • Plague (fleas on prairie dogs and rodents): keep pets away from colonies, flea control, don't handle dead rodents.
  • Salmonella from chickens: wash hands after birds and eggs (Chapter 5).
  • Rabies (skunks, bats): there is no treatment after symptoms start, and no shots without services. Keep dogs and cats vaccinated, keep kids away from wild animals, and scrub any bite for 15 minutes.

Power

Generators — safe use (CDC, FEMA)

  • Outdoors only, at least 20 feet from windows, doors, and vents. Never in the house, basement, garage, or barn with the door open.

Plains Weather & Disasters

Know the threats where we live and what to do in each.

Blizzards and extreme cold

  • Before: fill propane, stock firewood, food, water, medicines; charge everything; bring animals' water and feed in close.
  • During: stay inside. Plains blizzards cause whiteouts — people get lost between the house and barn. If you must go to the animals, tie a rope line from house to barn and go in pairs.
  • Never travel in a blizzard warning unless it's a life-or-death emergency.
  • Car winter kit: blankets/sleeping bags, hats, gloves, boots, water, snacks, shovel, ice scraper, sand/kitty litter, jumper cables, flashlight, phone charger, flares, and baby supplies when kids ride.
  • If stranded in a car: stay with the car, run the engine ~10 minutes an hour for heat with the tailpipe cleared of snow and a window cracked, tie bright cloth to the antenna. (NWS / Ready.gov)

Lessons from the December 2006 Holiday Blizzards

  • Two blizzards hit eastern Colorado about a week apart (Dec 20–22 and Dec 28–30). Drifts reached 10–15 feet; roads closed and homes were cut off; the National Guard and Civil Air Patrol searched for stranded drivers and dropped hay. (Colorado DHSEM after-action reports)
  • Thousands of cattle died (estimates of 8,000–15,000). Many ranchers had hay in the field but couldn't reach it through the drifts. In a 1997 storm, about 27,000–30,000 cattle died.
  • Lessons for us: expect a second storm; don't use up supplies in the first. Stage feed, hay, and water right next to the animals before the storm, not across a field. Keep a shovel at every door, and a sled or wagon to haul feed and water.

Tornadoes

  • Watch = conditions are right. Warning = take shelter now.
  • Go to the basement (or an interior room on the lowest floor without windows). Get under something sturdy; cover heads — bike helmets for kids help.
  • Keep shoes, a flashlight, radio, and baby bag in the shelter area.
  • Vehicles and mobile homes are not safe.

Hail and lightning

  • Hail can be big here — stay inside away from windows; protect gardens with hail cloth; park under cover.
  • "When thunder roars, go indoors." Wait 30 minutes after the last thunder. (NWS)

Grass and wildfire

  • On Red Flag Warning days (dry + windy), no outdoor burning, welding, or driving through dry grass.
  • Defensible space: mow and clear dry grass and weeds around the house, coop, hutches, propane tank, and woodpile.
  • Evacuate early if officials say go — wind-driven grass fires move very fast. Know two ways out.
  • Plan how to move or release animals.

Flooding (South Platte and ditches)

  • Turn around, don't drown. 6 inches of moving water can knock an adult down; 12 inches can carry away most cars. (NWS)
  • Move to higher ground if water rises. Keep kids away from floodwater (sewage, chemicals).
  • After flooding, treat well water as contaminated until tested (Chapter 2).

Extreme heat and drought

  • See "Summer heat" in Chapter 3. Water for people and animals first. Shade for rabbits and chickens (rabbits die from heat quickly).

Do it now

  • Car winter kits in every vehicle.
  • Shelter spot in the basement stocked (helmets, shoes, light, radio, baby bag).
  • Mow defensible space around buildings and propane tank.
  • Rope for a house-to-barn line.

  • The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints — "All Is Safely Gathered In," Longer-Term Food Supply, and the one-year basics list (400 lb grains, 60 lb legumes, etc. per adult; child percentages); BYU Department of Nutrition, Dietetics & Food Science shelf-life research. churchofjesuschrist.org/topics/food-storage
  • The Prepared (theprepared.com) — winter survival and lessons from the 2021 Texas blackout (tent-in-a-room, body heat, diesel gelling, indoor-rated heaters only).
  • Joel Salatin / Polyface Farm — the Raken (rabbit-over-chicken) house and deep-bedding method (8–12+ inches of carbon). Mother Earth News; McMurray Hatchery blog.
  • Colorado Division of Homeland Security & Emergency Management — after-action reports on the December 2006 Holiday Blizzards; Natural Hazards Center (CU Boulder) Quick Response Report 191 on livestock losses.
  • Recommended books for the shelf (classic, widely respected references): SAS Survival Handbook (John "Lofty" Wiseman); When Technology Fails (Matthew Stein) — off-grid water, heat, and food; Storey's Guide to Raising Chickens (Gail Damerow); Storey's Guide to Raising Rabbits (Bob Bennett); The Encyclopedia of Country Living (Carla Emery).

Medical training

  • American Red Cross and American Heart Association — First Aid, CPR (adult/child/infant). Stop the Bleed — bleeding control. 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. Poison Control 1-800-222-1222.
